Remark

It is worth observing that the very first gradient step
of PPO (for which ρt = 1 everywhere) recovers
the policy gradient with advantages of VPG from
§4.2.3, even though the derivation went through the
performance difference lemma rather than the policy
gradient theorem.
Also, while we can re-use the same batch of data
collected by a previous policy to update the current
one, we still mostly refer to PPO as an on-policy
method, since the surrogate objective is only valid
for policies that are close enough to the one that
produced the data.
Finally, the attentive reader may have noticed that
the number of gradient steps performed per PPO
iteration is not K but K × num_minibatches: at each
epoch, the collected batch is shuffled and split into
minibatches, and one gradient step is taken on each.
This allows for even more updates per batch, with
the clipping ratio making it possible.

Remark

There are many implementation details specific to a
given PPO codebase (or any deep RL algorithm) that
we have intentionally omitted. A few worth noting:
• Defining a collection period. Should we collect
N full episodes or T timesteps before each update? Fixing the number of episodes is closer
to the textbook definition, but the wall-clock
cost per update fluctuates as episode lengths
shift with the policy. Fixing T timesteps is
more common as it gives a constant compute
budget per iteration and works more naturally
with vectorized environments, at the price of
having to bootstrap V (sT ) on the last (truncated) step.
• Termination vs. truncation. A terminal state
has V (sT ) = 0, whereas a time-limit cutoff
requires bootstrapping with the critic’s estimate. Some implementations treat truncations
as terminations.
