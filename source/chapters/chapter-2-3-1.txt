Remark

In the previous example, the value function V π took
the form of an array, which was quite natural given
the discrete nature of the state. In environments
where the state is continuous, one natural solution
is to discretize it and we are back to the previous
case. Another solution, which is more common in
modern RL, is to use a function approximator. We
will develop this idea later in this book.
But how do we actually compute V π (s)? If you tried
the previous example by hand, you probably found
that it involves some kind of recurrence. Let us look
at one specific cell to make this explicit. Consider

V π (3, 2), the cell just left of the goal, under the original policy π (right-or-down with equal probability).
What can we say about it?
From this cell, π picks “right” or “down” with probability 1/2 each:
• If it picks “right”, the agent reaches the goal: it
collects a reward of −1 and the episode ends,
so this branch gives a total future reward of
−1.
• If it picks “down”, the action is blocked (we are
already on the bottom row): the agent stays
in place, collects −1, and finds itself in the
very same state (3, 2), from which the same
expected future awaits. This branch therefore
gives a total future reward of −1 + γV π (3, 2).
The weight γ is there because the reward of
−1 is collected immediately, while the future
reward V π (3, 2) is collected one step later, and
thus discounted by γ (we took γ = 1 in the
example).

This reframing using the Bellman equation is what
unifies all value-based methods. They all try to
estimate V π or Qπ by traversing, in some way, the
backup diagram. The traversal is characterized by
two questions:
• How deep? Do we expand the tree all the way
down to the end of the episode, or do we stop
early and replace the rest by a current estimate
of V π ?
• How wide? At each branching point, do we
follow a single path by sampling, or do we expand all branches and average them with their
probabilities? i.e. do we sample the expectation or compute it exactly?
Different combinations of answers to these two questions yield different families of algorithms. The remainder of this chapter is organized around this idea:
