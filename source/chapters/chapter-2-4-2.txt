We can in fact have a good intuition about this formula. For a trajectory τ that is sampled and from

• ∇θ log πθ (At | St ) is the direction in the space

• we sum these directions over all timesteps of

were to take a step toward that direction, τ
would now be more likely). So this is a R|θ|
vector, where |θ| is the number of parameters.
• the resulting vector is scaled by the trajectory
return G(τ ), a single scalar. If the trajectory
was good (G(τ ) large and positive), we step in
the direction that makes all of its actions more
likely. If it was bad, we step in the opposite
direction.

A note on the “loss” in code.
In a typical PyTorch implementation of SPG, you will
see something like:
loss = -(policy(obs).log_prob(act)*weight).mean()

where obs and act are the states and actions of the
sampled trajectories, weight is G(τ ) for SPG. The
gradient will be computed by torch.autograd.
87

This quantity is called a “loss” by analogy with supervised learning, but it should not be considered a
loss in the usual sense:
• its numerical value carries no information
about how well the policy performs. While
grad(loss) will indeed point in the opposite
direction of ∇θ J(θ), this does not imply that
the value of loss is J(θ).
• Even as a gradient signal, it is only valid at
the parameters that were used to collect the data.
The trajectories (obs, act) stored in the batch
were sampled from the old policy, and the policy gradient theorem requires the expectation
to be taken under that same policy. So for the
very first gradient step on this batch, the estimate is correct. But if we were to take a second
gradient step on the same batch, θ would have
moved and grad(loss) would no longer estimate ∇θ J(θ). This makes policy gradient
methods inherently on-policy, which is part of
why they tend to be less data-efficient than the
off-policy Q-learning methods of the previous
chapter (no replay buffer possible).
