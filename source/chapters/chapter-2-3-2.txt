There is an important remark to make about this formulation, regarding the interleaving of evaluation
and improvement. In the pseudocode, the policy is
updated right after each Q update (and so, equivalently, at the end of every episode). This does not
respect the strict separation of evaluation and improvement steps as in DP, where we first evaluate
the policy to convergence, then improve it.
In other words, we never wait for Qπk to be properly
estimated before moving to πk+1 . As soon as a new
return is observed, Q is updated and π is immediately made ϵ-greedy with respect to the new Q. The
next episode is then collected under this new policy.

56

Taking into account the new experience right away
allows the policy to improve faster.
This also justifies why we use a constant learning
rate α rather than 1/N (s). Since π keeps evolving,
returns collected long ago were generated by a policy
that no longer matches the current one. Averaging
them with equal weight, as 1/N (s) would do, would
bias the estimate toward the past. A constant α gives
more weight to recent returns, which is desirable as
the policy itself is a moving target.
The Monte Carlo Control approach has two drawbacks. First, it only works for episodic tasks: we
need episodes to terminate before we can compute
returns. Second, the variance of the returns can be
very high, especially for long episodes, which makes
the estimates noisy and thus slow to converge.
These drawbacks will be addressed in the next family
of methods. What if, instead of waiting until the
end of the episode to obtain a return, we used our
current estimate of V π to approximate the rest of the
trajectory?

SARSA is an instance of Temporal-Difference (TD)
learning. TD is similar to Monte Carlo in that it
relies on sampling a single path through the backup
diagram, but it differs in that it does not wait until
the end of the episode to update. Instead, it goes only
one step deep and uses the current estimate of V π to
fill in the rest. This is referred to as bootstrapping.
Concretely, after observing a single transition
(St , At , Rt+1 , St+1 ), we update our estimate of
V (St ) toward the value the Bellman equation
predicts, using V (St+1 ) as a replacement for the

Example: MC vs. TD on random walk
To appreciate the differences between MC and TD,
let us look at the 19-state random walk (Sutton
et al. (2018), Example 6.2 extended to 19 states). The
agent starts in the middle of a chain of 19 states and
moves left or right uniformly at random. Reaching
the leftmost end gives a reward of −1, reaching the
rightmost end gives +1, all other transitions give 0.
