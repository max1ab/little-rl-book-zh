Self-play

Because Go is a two-player game, the agent needs an
opponent to play against to collect experience. AlphaGo Zero uses self-play by playing against another
version of itself. It also has the benefit that the agent
always faces an opponent of its own level, providing
some sort of curriculum learning.
Note that this self-play does not degenerate into a
suboptimal strategy:

• the two-player zero-sum perfect-information
structure ensures any exploitable weakness on
one side is punished by the symmetric opponent.

Conclusion
Overview of the book
After some definitions (Chapter 1), we organized the
methods by what they try to learn (Chapter 2). In
the value-based family (Chapter 3), we saw different
ways of traversing the same Bellman backup diagram,
parameterized by two questions: how deep and how
wide. In the policy-based family (Chapter 4), we
instead tried to estimate the policy gradient with low
variance, and how to safely take more than one step
per batch of experience.
Finally, we looked at two popular instances of RL
methods (Chapter 5, Chapter 6), and tried to link
them to the concepts we built in the previous chapters.

139

Recurring patterns
To end the book, it is worth mentioning a few patterns that surfaced more than once across chapters.
Credit assignment. Every RL algorithm provides
a way of attributing credit through time: bootstrapping in TD, returns in MC, the policy gradient theorem in policy optimization.
Bias and variance. They appear in the choice between MC and TD, in the depth of n-step returns, in
the λ of GAE, in the importance ratio of PPO, in the
group baseline of GRPO. Every estimator in RL faces
this trade-off.
The data distribution moves. This is what fundamentally separates RL from supervised learning.
The agent generates its own training data, and any
update changes which data it will see next. Design
choices like trust regions manage this drift.
On-policy vs off-policy. How stale is the data
relative to the current policy? Q-learning tolerates
arbitrary staleness by decoupling the behavior policy
from the target policy, while PPO and GRPO use trust
regions. It implies practical limitations in modern RL
infrastructures.

What this book does not cover
Many active areas of RL were left out in this book,
including:
• Offline RL: learning a policy from a fixed
dataset without further interaction (CQL, IQL,
behavior cloning baselines).
• Deterministic policy gradients: DDPG,
TD3, SAC, and the off-policy actor-critic line
of work behind modern robotics.
• Model-based RL: Dreamer, MuZero, PlaNet,
and learned world models for planning.
• Exploration: multi-armed bandits, intrinsic
motivation (RND, ICM), Thompson sampling,
posterior sampling . . .
• Multi-agent RL: general-sum games, equilibrium concepts, opponent modeling.
• Inverse RL: recovering rewards from demonstrations.
• Hierarchical RL: options, sub-goals, temporal abstraction.

Going further
There are many resources to dive deeper into RL.
Among the citations of this book, you can notably
